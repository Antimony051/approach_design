3. Design Approach
PaveWatch uses a supervised unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV) to inspect paved surfaces. It uses a two-dimensional LiDAR and a camera to collect the required data and processes the data to generate findings. This section starts with the two overall design options in 3.1. We then provide the system overview in 3.2, the six subsystems in 3.3, and finally the Level 2 prototype in Section 3.4. The whole design hinges around providing the data processing pipeline with the best possible data, so capturing data with enough fidelity and positional and temporal accuracy is the main driver behind the design. Furthermore, the $1,000 budget limit also places hard constraints on the whole design; a weight limit also must be considered for all items, so that we are light enough to fly. The constraints raised by the laws around flying are there but not felt as much because these are mostly solving problems in the wider industry. Relevant standards to follow include IEEE 802.3-2022 for Ethernet, RFC 7946 for geographic exports [14], and RFC 8446/TLS 1.3 for secure network transfer of collected data [15].
3.1 Design Options
We considered two ways to carry the PaveWatch sensing equipment: a supervised drone flying above the pavement and a supervised wheeled rover driving along it. Both options follow similar data collection and processing. They collect LiDAR cross-sections, camera images, and timestamped position and orientation data, then save the data onboard for post processing. The main difference is how each vehicle reaches the pavement and collects data of sufficient quality. The following sections explain the two options and the reasons for our choice.
3.1.1 Drone-based pavement inspection
The drone approach uses a multirotor UAV to carry the sensors, a camera and a LiDAR, both pointed down toward the ground. The flight controller handles the aircraft’s movement, while a Raspberry Pi is used as the data collection computer. The operator then supervises the flight and can take manual control to go around obstacles. As the drone moves along the route, it collects LiDAR data and images. Synchronized position and orientation estimates allow these observations to be aligned during processing. The raw data is stored on the board. After the run is over, the data is transferred to a data processing pipeline to identify surface concerns and their locations. We are planning around a height of 5–8 m and a speed of 0.25–1 m/s.
The main benefit of the drone is that it can inspect pavement without having to drive over the defects it is measuring. It can also cross gaps and surface obstacles along a route. Viewing the pavement from above also allows it to cover a wider strip in each pass. These benefits come with limits on payload weight and battery energy, since the aircraft must keep itself and the sensing equipment in the air. Wind, vibration, and changes in attitude can also introduce noise into measurements. We therefore need to test flight stability and endurance with the payload installed, along with sensor timing and calibration. Yet we may be unable to remove all uncertainty and noise from the collection process, so we need to apply clever post processing on the data to get the most out of it. This is the approach we chose.
3.1.2 Rover-based pavement inspection
The rover approach places the sensing equipment on a battery-powered wheeled rover. Motor drivers and a motion controller move the vehicle, while wheel encoders and an inertial sensor help track its movement. A rigid mount holds the camera and LiDAR in a fixed relationship. The camera looks at the pavement ahead of or between the wheels, and the LiDAR scans across the direction of travel. The mounting height and angle must keep the pavement within the scanner’s usable measurement range. As with the drone, an operator supervises the route and can stop the vehicle or take manual control. Wheel motion and inertial measurements, supported by available GNSS or surveyed reference points, provide estimates of position and orientation. The Pi saves the synchronized data for the same processing and reporting workflow used by the drone.
Because the rover can carry a lot more mass, it could support longer collection runs without needing to be recharged. Its shorter viewing distance could also provide more image detail and make repeat measurements easier. However, the rover must be able to drive through the area it inspects. Wheel clearance, traction, and slope limit where it can go, and curbs, debris, or deep defects may require the operator to go around it. Uneven pavement can tilt the chassis, and the sensors’ frame of reference, while wheel slip can make the encoder estimates drift. For this reason, independent position and orientation checks are still needed. The design would also need a tested stopping response and a plan for handling pedestrians and obstacles.
3.2 System Overview
At a basic level, PaveWatch flies a supervised route over a paved surface, records LiDAR scans and camera images of the pavement, and turns that data into inspection reports after landing. Figure 3-1 shows this as a simple black box. The inputs are the operator’s commands for the route and run, pilot takeover through the RC handset, power from the flight battery, the pavement surface the sensors observe, and position references from GPS and survey control points. The outputs are live system status for the operator, the survey data recorded onboard, and inspection reports that show each finding with its images, measurements, and location.

During a survey, the flight controller keeps the aircraft stable and on route, and the pilot can take over at any time. The recorder saves every LiDAR scan and camera image along with the aircraft’s position and time, since a measurement is only useful to us if we know where it was taken. After landing, we copy the data off the aircraft for processing into findings, which are then stored and reviewed as reports [3], [5].
3.2.1 Microcontroller and Microcomputer
PaveWatch uses two computers on the aircraft, and each one has a different job. The flight controller runs the autopilot firmware and keeps the aircraft stable and on route, and we cover its options together with the airframe in Section 3.3.1. The second computer is the recorder, which receives the LiDAR packets over Ethernet, the camera frames, and the position messages from the flight controller, checks for dropped data, and writes everything to storage during the flight.

3.3 Subsystems
Our design keeps the six subsystems defined in the project charter. The flight platform, power, and control subsystem handles aircraft movement and power distribution. Diagnostics and telemetry monitors hardware health and streams status updates to the ground, while sensor acquisition collects and stores LiDAR and camera data. Ground control and communications manages mission uploads, radio links, and operator commands. Data processing and change detection reconstructs pavement geometry and identifies surface defects, and reporting, database, and alerts organizes confirmed findings alongside their visual and geometric evidence.

This subsystem carries the sensor payload, flies supervised pavement survey legs, accepts pilot takeover, and supplies time-stamped position and attitude data to the processing system. Flight testing is planned at 5–8 m above ground level and 0.25–1 m/s [1], [3], [16].

Flight energy and payload installation
The aircraft uses a 4S flight battery with one branch for propulsion and control and a separate branch for the payload.
The LiDAR and computer branches each get their own protection and regulation. The picoScan (LiDAR) documentation lists a 9–30 V supply, and the computer, camera, storage, and cooling loads share one dedicated regulated rail [8], [9].
Figure 3-5 shows the intended branch separation.
Fig. 3-5. Proposed power branches.
For each load, the energy estimate uses active and idle states, time, and converter efficiency. With current I, voltage V, duration t in hours, active duty fraction d, and branch efficiency η, the planning model is:
E_load = V × [I_active × d + I_idle × (1 − d)] × t / η.    (3-1)
 A 4S 5.2 Ah battery has 14.8 V × 5.2 Ah = 76.96 Wh nominal energy. For screening, assume calibrated usable energy is 80% of nominal (61.57 Wh); preserving 20% of that usable energy leaves 49.25 Wh for flight. The SunnySky APC 10 × 4.7 reference curve at 14.8 V implies about 82 W per motor near 563 gf, or roughly 326 W for propulsion. Adding an illustrative 20 W for avionics/payload gives about 346 W total and an estimated 8.5 min [16].
For a 2.25 kg all-up design target, hover requires 2.25 × 9.81 / 4 = 5.52 N, or about 563 gf per motor. A 2:1 static thrust-to-weight screening ratio requires at least 1,125 gf per motor. SunnySky's 880 KV X2216 data for an APC 10 × 4.7 propeller on 14.8 V reports about 1,200 gf at 17.4 A and 257.5 W per motor.

The Pocket display will use short, readable warnings such as LOW BATTERY, LIDAR LOST, CAMERA LOST, STORAGE LOW, and POSE INVALID. A HealthMonitor on the recorder will check sensor activity, storage space, and the availability of position and orientation data. 3.3.3 Sensor Payload and Data Acquisition
In this subsystem we record LiDAR scans and camera images during each survey and timestamp every scan and image, so that processing can match them to the aircraft’s position and orientation at the time they were captured and place them on the pavement. The two sensors cover different defects. The LiDAR measures the shape of the pavement, which is what we need to find depressions and height changes, while the camera captures the surface detail needed to find cracks, which can be narrower than the spacing between LiDAR points. Our LiDAR is a SICK picoScan150 Pro that we received through the SICK $10K Challenge, so we did not select it ourselves, but we have already confirmed that it streams scans over Ethernet. For the camera, we chose the Raspberry Pi Camera Module 3 based on the comparison in Table 3-4. Both sensors connect to the Raspberry Pi 4 recorder from Section 3.2.1, and the Pi also reads the aircraft pose from the flight controller through its MAVLink telemetry messages [3], [9], [20].
The picoScan150 Pro is mounted so its 276° scan plane points straight down and across the route. Each sweep measures one slice of the pavement, and the slices build up the surface as the aircraft moves forward. The sensor can scan at 15 to 50 Hz with an adjustable angular resolution [20], and for planning we used about 50 Hz at 0.25° to 0.33° with our survey speed of 0.25 to 1 m/s. From 5 m, a rough calculation suggests the points are about 22 to 29 mm apart across the route and the scans are about 5 to 20 mm apart along it, which gives more than ten points across a 0.5 m depression and at least 25 scans along it. Whether the sensor offers this exact profile is not confirmed yet, so we will pick the closest available setting and check the spacing and its typical 20 mm range error in our tests at 5, 6.5, and 8 m [3], [4], [20].
Table 3-4 lists the three cameras we compared for the survey images. The InnoMaker U20CAM-9281M was our first plan because it has a global shutter, which captures the whole frame at once and avoids the skew a rolling shutter produces while the camera moves, and that mattered when we planned to fly at 2 to 3 m/s. When we worked out the image detail, though, its 1 MP sensor and 148° lens gave roughly 15 to 25 mm per pixel from 5 m, so a 20 mm crack would cover about one pixel. The Raspberry Pi Camera Module 3 gives about 1.4 mm per pixel over a 6.5 m × 3.7 m footprint from the same height, or about 14 pixels across the same crack. At our survey speed of 0.25 to 1 m/s, its rolling shutter shifts the image by only about 17 to 68 mm during readout, which is under 2% of the frame, and since that shift is even across the image, processing can correct it using the known speed. The Camera Module 3 also connects directly to the Pi's camera port and costs about $25, so we chose it [21], [22], [23]. Figure 3-7 shows the LiDAR and the camera.

Our plan is to capture three images per second. S4 flags any gap in the data longer than one second, and earlier planning targeted 70 to 80% overlap between images. At our survey speed, overlap is easy to meet. At 1 m/s and 5 m, two images per second already give about 87% overlap, and three give about 91%. The frame rate is set by the gap requirement instead, since a gap is measured in time rather than distance. At two images per second, a single dropped frame puts the gap right at the one-second limit no matter how slowly we fly, so any small delay would trip it, while at three per second it leaves only about 0.67 s. The Pi, not the camera, limits the rate, since a published Pi 4 benchmark encoded about 5 JPEGs per second at 5 MP one at a time and about 15 per second in parallel [26], which scales to roughly 2 and 6 per second at our 11.9 MP, so three per second should only be possible with parallel encoding. A bench trial will confirm the rate, along with an exposure of 1 ms or less to keep motion blur under 1 mm and focus locked at survey height. We will also run a ten-minute bench recording with both sensors to confirm the data has no gaps.
Each JPEG should be about 3 to 5 MB, so at three per second the camera writes about 9 to 15 MB/s. The LiDAR and pose log add less than 1 MB/s, which brings a ten-minute flight to about 6 to 10 GB. Table 3-5 compares the three ways we considered storing this data, with each option priced including the hardware it needs. Writing to the Pi's own card slot is the cheapest, but the slot is limited to about 50 MB/s and the survey data would share a card with the operating system. A Samsung T7 SSD avoids both of those problems, but it is much faster than we need, costs about twice as much, and adds about 54 g. We chose a SanDisk Extreme 128 GB card in a USB 3 reader. It gives about four to six times the write speed we expect and holds 12 to 20 flights while weighing only about 4 g. Since it keeps up with every camera setting we are considering, the frame rate trial will not change this choice. Both the card and the reader will be strapped down so vibration cannot loosen them, and each card's sustained write speed will be measured before use [24], [27], [28].

Acquisition software, timing, and survey package
The recorder software is split into the parts shown in Figure 3-8. RecorderSupervisor starts and stops each run, and LidarCapture, CameraCapture, and PoseLogger each save one data stream with its own timestamps and sequence numbers. HealthMonitor logs any gap or fault, ManifestWriter records the run and calibration details, and ExportService copies the finished package off the recorder for processing after landing.
Fig. 3-8. Proposed acquisition component connections and data products.
We chose Python for the recorder because the official libraries for both sensors and the flight controller support it. ROS 2 was the other framework we considered, since it offers a ready-made recorder and live visualization, but it is built for Ubuntu while the camera library is designed for Raspberry Pi OS, so using both would mean building one of them from source. ROS 2 is also meant for sharing live data between programs, and we only record files and process them after landing. C++ would be faster, but the heavy work of decoding scans and encoding images already runs in compiled code inside these libraries, and our timing comes from the sensors’ own timestamps. Each sensor runs as its own program, so if LiDAR capture drops data in testing, we can move just that part to C++ [25], [26], [29].
Every flight produces one survey package. It holds the original LiDAR data, the images with a file of their timestamps and settings, a pose log, an event log, and a summary file with the run details, calibration, and checksums. Each capture part writes through a fixed-size queue so that a slow status update cannot hold up a sensor, and any dropped packet, dropped frame, or failed write is logged as a gap. A normal stop marks the package complete, while a power loss leaves it marked incomplete, so processing does not mistake a partial run for a full one.
The recorder keeps one common clock and saves each device's own timestamps alongside it. The LiDAR's clock is not synchronized when it starts, so we plan to sync it to the recorder over a network time protocol (PTP or NTP), and for the camera we use the sensor's timestamps instead of the time a frame reaches our code. SA2 allows 20 ms of timing error over at least ten minutes, which is about 20 mm of travel at 1 m/s, and we will check it with an independent timing test rather than relying on the timestamps alone [1], [4], [20].

Before each flight, the operator creates a survey route and checks the planned waypoints, altitude, and flight limits. The mission is then uploaded to the flight controller using MAVLink. After uploading, the system reads the mission back to verify that it matches the original plan. 3.3.5 Data Processing and Change Detection
The Data Processing and Change Detection subsystem turns the recorded LiDAR scans and camera images into pavement findings. The recorded survey data is transferred from the onboard recorder to an offboard data processing pipeline for analysis. The subsystem reconstructs the measured surface, identifies areas that may need attention, and passes the findings and supporting evidence to reporting. Comparing surveys of the same area also provides a way to track pavement changes over time.

Post-flight processing also allows updated methods to be rerun on the original survey data. Both local and cloud deployment support the same processing workflow and GeoJSON handoff to reporting.
Before identifying surface features, the subsystem checks whether the recorded data is usable. This includes checking for missing records, readable files, timestamps, etc. Each scan and image needs to be associated with the drone’s position and orientation when it was captured. Records with missing or unreliable information are flagged so a gap in the data does not become a misleading pavement measurement.
The drone’s movement must also be accounted for before individual scans can form a useful surface model. Each LiDAR scan measures distances relative to the sensor, so simply joining the scans together would ignore changes in the drone’s position and tilt. The sensor’s mounting position and angle, together with the recorded drone position and orientation, place the measurements in a common coordinate system. This allows scans collected at different moments to describe the same stretch of pavement [1]. Table 3-8 compares the pose and reference approaches.

Processing workflow
Figure 3-10 shows the processing components and their connections. ImportValidator checks the survey package described in Section 3.3.3, including its manifest, file checksums, and completion status. An interrupted recording remains marked incomplete even when some of its data is usable.
Fig. 3-10. Proposed processing components, including independent geometry and image branches.
TimeMapper relates the sensor timestamps to the common recording reference, while PoseResolver associates each observation with the drone’s position and orientation at capture time. The timing information supplied by acquisition is retained because the camera captures an image before the recorder receives it, and the drone may move during that delay. Observations without a usable reference are flagged and excluded from quantitative analysis, as required by S6 [1].
FrameTransformer then applies the sensor mounting calibration and places the measurements in the survey frame. GeometryAnalyzer and VisualDetector process the LiDAR and image data separately. FindingAssembler associates compatible results, and ReportExporter passes the findings and evidence links to reporting.
Surface analysis and change detection
Geometry analysis uses the surrounding pavement as a local reference to identify broad raised areas, depressions, and vertical steps. This accounts for normal road slope when measuring a feature’s height or depth. Evaluated sloped regions also include rise-over-run measurements to support the surface-gradient requirement [1].
The image branch identifies visible concern candidates and is evaluated using labeled examples. Image findings and geometric measurements are associated only when their timing, position, and coverage support a match. Image-only findings remain available when matching geometry is missing. Evaluation reports results from image analysis alone and findings supported by both image and LiDAR analysis separately, where matching observations are available [1].
When an earlier survey is available, change detection compares observations of the same pavement in a common coordinate frame. Alignment quality and measurement uncertainty are checked because a shift between datasets could appear to be a surface change. Areas without usable coverage in both surveys are marked unavailable for comparison rather than unchanged.
Coordinate alignment and measurement checks
All measurements use a common local survey frame with a defined origin, axis directions, units, and orientation convention. Separate coordinate frames define the reference points and axis directions used for the aircraft, navigation sensor, LiDAR, and camera. Measurements are converted from these individual frames into the common local survey frame before they are combined [1].
For each LiDAR point, processing first applies the sensor-to-aircraft calibration, which describes the sensor’s mounting position and angle. The aircraft’s position and orientation at capture time then place the point in the survey frame. Camera observations use the camera’s mounting position and angle, along with calibration values for focal length, optical center, and lens distortion. Each transformation has a defined direction and calibration version. Figure 3-11 shows this chain.
Fig. 3-11. Pose and calibration transform chain.
The LiDAR measures distance along each laser beam. Processing uses the beam direction, the aircraft’s position and orientation, and the sensor’s mounting position and angle to place each measured point in the survey frame. Placing findings on a geographic map requires a documented conversion from local survey coordinates to geographic coordinates.
Table 3-9 summarizes the main error sources and their checks. Final accuracy depends on their combined effects.
Table 3-9. Measurement and alignment checks.
[TABLE]
Contribution | Evidence needed | Consequence if invalid
LiDAR range and target conditions | Known targets at representative distances and surface conditions | Flag or exclude affected measurements
Clock offset, drift, and capture timing | Independent timing checks over at least ten minutes | Reject unsupported time associations
Position, orientation, and interpolation | Known-pose tests and independent reference measurements | Limit quantitative use or improve alignment
Camera and mounting calibration | Camera parameters and measured sensor positions and angles | Check calibration again after mounting changes
Geographic registration | Independent checkpoints and documented origin, orientation, scale, and datum | Retain local results and flag uncertain map placement

Processing results are checked against independent reference measurements to evaluate alignment, detection, and measurement accuracy against the project requirements [1], [3].
The processing pipeline submits findings as GeoJSON to the reporting subsystem’s data ingestion API, including evidence references and data-quality warnings.
3.3.6 Reporting, Database, and Alerts
The Reporting, Database, and Alerts subsystem is a cloud-based web app (the web portal) that turns the processed survey results into something users can explore and share. A user logs into the portal and browses the findings on an interactive dashboard, with an option to export a PDF report [1], [2].
The web app receives the findings from the processing subsystem as a GeoJSON file (a standard map file format, defined in RFC 7946). Each finding has an identifier, a defect type, a position, measurements with their units, the software version that produced it, quality warnings, and links to its images and 3D scan evidence. Positions are given as distances in meters from the drone’s starting point, plus a best-effort GPS location where one is available.
The dashboard focuses on how many potholes were found, where they are, and warnings about the data. A map shows the potholes as markers at their locations. Summary numbers show how many potholes were found and how many there are of each defect type (such as potholes and cracks). Filters narrow the findings by type, date, or warning, and clicking a finding opens its images, measurements, and 3D scan evidence. Users can also compare a survey with an earlier one to see which defects are new or have changed over time.
The warnings matter because a survey cannot find a pothole in an area it did not measure well. If part of the path was not scanned, or the drone’s position data was bad, that area has no findings, so on the map it looks the same as an area that truly has no potholes. The warnings mark these areas, so users can tell “no potholes found” from “not enough data to know.” They cover parts of the path that were not scanned, missing or bad drone position data, scan and image data that may not line up, and areas with no 3D scan data.
Users can click Export at any time to create a PDF report of a survey. The report is a short summary: the number of potholes found, a map with their locations, each pothole’s photo and measurements, and the areas with data warnings. Figure 3-12 shows this reporting workflow.
Table 3-10 compares the ways the results can be delivered to users. The current choice is the cloud web app, because it gives shared access from anywhere and lets the processing computer send results directly. This choice may change after testing. The local app is kept as a secondary option for use without internet in the field, and the portable report is a feature of the app itself.
Table 3-10. Delivery options for the reporting subsystem.
[TABLE]
Option | Benefit | Tradeoff | Role in Design
Portable report (PDF or HTML file) | Easy to share and review offline | No searching or comparing across surveys | Secondary: exported from the app

Hosted web app (cloud database and file storage) | Shared access and remote processing | Needs secure login, internet, data storage rules, ongoing cost, and a server to set up | Primary: current choice
[/TABLE]
Behind the dashboard, the findings are stored in a PostgreSQL database (a widely used database that runs on a server) with PostGIS (an add-on that lets the database store and search map locations). The images and 3D scan evidence are kept in cloud file storage. Each file is checked before it is saved. A file in the wrong format is rejected with an error and has to be corrected and sent again. 3.4 Level 2 Prototype Design
The Level 2 design brings the six subsystems together into the aircraft, the equipment used by the operator, and the software that turns the recorded data into findings. The main idea is to give each part a clear job: the flight controller flies the aircraft, the Raspberry Pi records the survey, and the offboard processing pipeline analyzes the data after collection. This section explains how these parts connect and how we plan to fit them onto the aircraft.
Figure 3-13 shows how power, commands, status updates, and survey data move through PaveWatch.
3.4.1 Level 2 Diagram and Interface Contracts
Fig. 3-13. Intended hardware and software integration, Level 2. 
The aircraft uses the MicoAir H743v2-AIO-35A flight controller and its four integrated ESC channels, following the configuration selected in Section 3.3.1. PX4 1.16.0 handles flight control and the configured failsafe responses, using position and heading information from the M10G GNSS and compass module. The ESCs drive four SunnySky X2216 880 KV motors with Master Airscrew MR 10 × 4.5-inch propellers. A 4S, 14.8 V nominal, 5,200 mAh battery supplies the aircraft. Separate rails supply the LiDAR and the recorder, with regulation matched to each load. 
The SICK picoScan150 Pro connects to the Raspberry Pi 4 over Ethernet, and the Raspberry Pi Camera Module 3 connects through the Pi’s CSI camera ribbon cable. The Pi also receives timestamped aircraft position, orientation, and flight status through the MAVLink serial connection to the flight controller. Survey data is written to the SanDisk Extreme 128 GB microSD card in a USB 3 reader, keeping it separate from the Pi’s operating-system card. Together, these connections give processing the surface observations and the timing and position records needed to relate them to the pavement [6], [9], [21], [27], [28].
The RadioMaster Pocket communicates with the RP1 V2 receiver over the ELRS radio link, carrying pilot commands to the aircraft and critical status information back to the handset. We intend to show battery warnings and payload faults on the Pocket so the operator can see them while holding the flight controls.  The ground-station laptop uses a separate MAVLink telemetry connection for mission upload, acknowledgements, and detailed status. 
On the Pi, the Python recorder software runs the LiDAR, camera, and pose capture tasks together. RecorderSupervisor coordinates the run, HealthMonitor records faults, and ManifestWriter saves the run details, calibration information, and file checksums. Each stream keeps its timing information, and missing packets, dropped images, or failed writes are recorded as gaps. A normal stop closes the survey package as complete; an interrupted run remains marked incomplete. This allows processing to use the available data without mistaking a partial survey for full coverage.
After landing, ExportService copies the survey package off the recorder and checks the transferred files. The offboard processing pipeline then aligns the observations, reconstructs the pavement surface, and identifies defects.  The data processing subsystem’s findings and evidence references go through the reporting subsystem’s data ingestion API to the web app, where PostgreSQL with PostGIS stores the findings and cloud file storage holds the supporting images and scan evidence. The user can then review the survey on the dashboard and export a PDF report. Data-quality warnings remain attached to the results throughout this process.
3.4.2 Mechanical Integration and User Access
We plan to mount the system on a custom 500 mm quadcopter frame made from carbon fiber plates and tubes, with PA6-CF printed parts for the mounts and supports. The Holybro X500 v2 design files serve as a dimensional reference. Building the frame around the payload gives us room to place the sensors near the aircraft’s center of gravity while leaving their view of the pavement clear [8], [17].
The LiDAR and camera share a removable, rigid plate below the aircraft. Both face downward, with the LiDAR scan plane running across the flight route. Keeping the sensors fixed relative to each other lets processing use a consistent mounting calibration. If vibration isolation is needed, it will support the plate as a whole so the sensors move together. The camera ribbon and LiDAR cables will be secured without pulling on their connectors, and any change to the mount will require the calibration to be checked again.
The Pi, storage reader, and power regulator sit together on the electronics tray under a lightweight, ventilated cover. We plan to use PETG or ASA for this cover and the cable guides, with labeled connectors and access for setup, data removal, and shutdown. The cover must protect the wiring while allowing heat to escape and leaving the sensor windows unobstructed. The GPS and compass module sits on a non-magnetic mast away from the battery and motor wiring. We are not claiming a dust or water protection rating for the assembled aircraft.
Our current layout reserves about 120 × 100 × 130 mm for the sensors and their connections, and 120 × 90 × 60 mm for the recorder assembly. The estimated payload mass is 440–550 g against a 650 g target. 
The intended design gives us a clear starting point for building PaveWatch, from collecting observations on the aircraft to presenting findings to the user. The next section explains how we will test the individual subsystems and then check their operation together. We will begin with ground tests of power, recording, timing, communications, and processing before moving to integrated flight trials, using the results to compare the prototype with our requirements and guide any changes needed in Capstone II.
