The Problem:
Sidewalks, roads, parking areas, and other paved surfaces gradually crack, heave, settle, and
erode because of factors such as tree-root growth, freeze-thaw cycles, loading, weather, and
normal wear. Because this deterioration often develops slowly, defects may go undocumented
until they become large enough to create safety hazards, increase maintenance costs, or
require more extensive repairs. The American Society of Civil Engineers (ASCE) gave U.S.
roads a D+ in its 2025 Report Card for America’s Infrastructure and estimated a national
infrastructure funding gap of approximately $3.7 trillion.

Surface deterioration is also a safety concern for pedestrians. According to the National Safety
Council and the National Council on Aging (NCOA), falls are the leading cause of injury-related
death among older adults, resulting in more than 43,000 deaths in 2024 and approximately 3.85
million emergency-room visits in 2023, with an estimated annual economic impact of roughly
$80 billion. Uneven or deteriorated walking surfaces can create trip hazards, making early
identification and tracking of pavement deformation important for maintenance planning and
public safety.

Current pavement inspection methods often depend on personnel visually examining large
areas and documenting defects manually. This process can be slow, labor-intensive, and
subject to human errors. It can also be difficult to determine whether a crack, settlement area, or
other defect has changed significantly between inspections when measurements are not
collected in a repeatable way. Industry reporting from DSLRpros indicates that drone-based
infrastructure inspection can reduce inspection time by as much as 70% and reduce costs by
approximately 40% to 60% compared with conventional inspection crews. These limitations
create a need for a repeatable inspection system that can collect consistent surface
measurements, compare conditions over time, and automatically identify areas that require
closer attention.

Proposed Solution:
The gap between what can be measured automatically and what is still commonly inspected
manually is where PaveWatch fits in. PaveWatch is a proposed autonomous unmanned aerial
vehicle (UAV) system designed to survey sidewalks, roads, parking areas, and other exposed
paved surfaces along repeatable flight paths. Its purpose is to detect, measure, and track
surface deformation such as cracking, heaving, settlement, and erosion over multiple inspection
dates.

For each survey, an operator defines the inspection area and uploads a pre-planned route. The
UAV then follows that route using GPS-based navigation while a sensing payload collects high-
resolution measurements of the pavement surface. After each flight, the collected data will be

offloaded to cloud servers for processing. The processing system will organize and align the
new survey data, generate a georeferenced representation of the inspected surface, and
compare it with historic survey data. Differences between the current and baseline
measurements will be used to identify changes in surface elevation, crack development,
settlement, or other measurable deformation.

The system will present the results through a user-accessible reporting interface. This allows
engineers or maintenance personnel to identify where deterioration is occurring, determine how
it is changing over time, and prioritize locations for further inspection or repair.

By automating repeatable data collection and comparison over time, PaveWatch is intended to
reduce the amount of manual inspection required, improve consistency between inspections,
provide objective records of surface condition, and help maintenance personnel identify
developing pavement problems before they become more severe.


Process Diagram:




The process begins by planning the mission and defining the survey area and flight path. The
drone then flies autonomously and collects sensor and GPS data. After the flight, the data is
transferred to a computer or server, where it is processed and compared with the stored
baseline survey. Any significant surface changes are identified and flagged. Finally, the results
are displayed in a report or dashboard and stored for future inspections.

System Diagram:




The system is split into six subsystems. The Flight Platform, Power & Control subsystem
handles autonomous flight, navigation, and power distribution, while the Sensor Payload & Data
Acquisition subsystem collects surface and imaging data. Diagnostics & Telemetry monitors
system health and sends status information to the ground station. The Ground Control &
Communications subsystem is used to plan missions, send commands, and monitor the drone.
After the flight, the collected data is sent to Data Processing & Change Detection, where it is
analyzed and compared with previous survey data. The final results are then sent to Reporting,
Database, & Alerts for storage, visualization, and notification of any detected changes.

Potential Obstacles:
Technically: The team may disagree on technical decisions such as sensor selection, hardware platforms,
software tools, or implementation approaches.
Financially: Budget limitations may create disagreements over purchasing decisions, example might be
buing a prebuilt component that will make the work easier but cost more.

equipment, and arranging times when team members are available may cause delays.
