1. Problem Statement
Paved surfaces require regular monitoring to identify deterioration before it becomes a safety or maintenance concern. Manual inspections can be labor-intensive and inconsistent, while image-based methods face limitations related to lighting and surface-depth measurement. These challenges complicate the collection of repeatable condition data. The following sections present the need for improved pavement-condition monitoring, the objective of PaveWatch, and relevant background on existing inspection approaches.
1.1. Need Statement
Paved surfaces such as sidewalks, parking areas and campus roads gradually develop cracks, potholes, settlement, and other surface distress because of traffic loading, moisture and drainage conditions [1]. These changes can reduce serviceability and create hazards, but they may be difficult to identify early or compare across inspection dates. An audit by the Los Angeles City Controller found that, over the five fiscal years preceding the audit, the city faced more than 1,700 sidewalk injury claims and 1,020 lawsuits, paying over $35 million in settlements. The City lacked a complete inventory of sidewalk repair needs, while a comprehensive assessment was estimated to cost over $10 million and take two to three years [2]. A 2019 New York City Comptroller audit of the Parks Department’s Trees & Sidewalks Program found an average wait of 101 days from a service request to inspection and 419 days from inspection to repair [3]. Current pavement-condition methods include manual surveys and 2D imaging, but these approaches can be labor-intensive, variable between inspections, and affected by environmental conditions or limitations in measuring surface depth [4], [5]. Maintenance organizations therefore need a practical method for collecting repeatable, location-referenced condition data and identifying changes that warrant further inspection or repair. This need is directly relevant to Mississippi State University, where Campus Services is responsible for sidewalks, parking facilities, roadways, paving, preventive maintenance, and accessibility improvements [6].
1.2. Objective Statement
The objective of PaveWatch is to provide an unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV) platform for repeatable inspection of paved areas, such as sidewalks and parking lots, and location-referenced reporting of their condition. An operator marks the survey area and flight path, and the UAV collects surface and position data along that path. PaveWatch combines multiple sensor inputs to address limitations of individual sensing methods. The system organizes the collected data and identifies candidate surface concerns, including raised or depressed areas, vertical discontinuities, and surface gradients. An earlier survey provides additional change information when available. Its report presents candidate concerns with supporting images and available geometric measurements. Designated municipal or campus maintenance personnel use the findings to decide which locations require further inspection or repair. PaveWatch is meant to support maintenance and engineering decisions, not to replace the judgment of a qualified inspectoror diagnose structural problems on its own.
1.3. Background and Related Work
Pavement condition depends on both the surface and the supporting soil. Moisture infiltration and inadequate drainage can weaken underlying materials, while expansive soils can swell as they absorb water and cause pavement heave or unevenness [1]. These mechanisms help explain the need for repeated monitoring as environmental conditions change. Existing assessment methods include standardized visual surveys, 2D and 3D imaging, and UAV photogrammetry [5], [7], [8]. Manual visual surveys are labor-intensive and subject to variability between inspectors. Two-dimensional imaging can document visible distress, but shadows, stains, poor contrast, and image resolution complicate interpretation, and these systems do not directly measure surface depth [5]. Three-dimensional imaging adds depth information through additional sensing equipment and processing [5]. UAV inspection also requires flight planning, safe site operations, and processing and interpretation of collected data [8], [9]. Automated collection thereforedoes not, by itself, eliminate the need to interpret and review inspection results. A formal patent search is still required before making any claim of novelty or patentability.
These needs guide the survey, surface-measurement, and reporting requirements in Section 2.
2. Design Requirement Specifications
PaveWatch inspects paved areas and provides location-referenced evidence of surface concerns for maintenance review. A survey identifies current surface geometry and visible distress; an earlier survey may provide additional change information but is not required for surface assessment. This section defines system performance, subsystem demonstrations, imposed constraints, and applicable standards. Subsystem boundaries follow the signed team charter [10]; Section 1 defines the project need, and the development plan provides technical context [11].
The prototype addresses a controlled sidewalk or parking-area segment in dry daylight, with a proposed survey envelope of 5–8 m above the pavement at 1–2 m/s. Initial geometry assessment concerns broad raised or depressed areas, vertical discontinuities, and surface gradients. Fine crack-width measurement, subsurface condition, and structural diagnosis remain outside the acceptance scope. Numerical limits are proposed acceptance targets, not measured performance or confirmed customer commitments.

For S2, the acceptance set includes at least 10 geometric-feature cases and 10 regions without qualifying features, excluded from parameter tuning. Raised areas, depressions, and vertical discontinuities are represented. Recall is the fraction of reference features detected; precision is the fraction of reported candidates that correspond to reference features.
For S3, height difference means the elevation difference between the feature and adjacent reference pavement within the same survey. A vertical discontinuity uses the surfaces on either side of the step; an optional historical comparison instead measures the difference between surveys. Reference height uncertainty is ≤10 mm. Calibration, timing, aircraft motion, and range error all contribute to the final errors. Evaluation targets are independent of any controls used for alignment; accuracy and repeatability checks follow pavement-data quality principles [4].
For S6, position includes three coordinates and attitude includes roll, pitch, and yaw or an equivalent orientation representation. Record frame origin, axes, units, orientation convention, time reference, and sensor-to-aircraft mounting transforms. Position and orientation may be interpolated to acquisition times using the recorded timing relationship. Independent known-position/orientation checks demonstrate that the reference is used correctly.

C1. Small-UAS operating category. These operating conditions are relevant because they define the aircraft and flight procedures that can be used for physical evaluation. The total takeoff mass limit of <55 lb, altitude limit of ≤400 ft above ground, visual-line-of-sight requirement, pilot qualifications, airspace permissions, registration, and Remote ID conditions affect platform selection and flight planning. We will account for the complete installed payload when assessing aircraft mass and confirm the applicable permissions and operating requirements for the selected aircraft and site before flight.

C2. Flight-weather limits. Weather imposes practical limits on both flight control and data quality. Wind gusts may push the aircraft off its planned route, while rain or fog may interfere with image visibility and sensor measurements. The Part 107 visibility and cloud-separation values in the table are legal minima; they do not by themselves ensure safe flight or useful survey data. We will assess weather before each flight and conduct accepted tests in dry daylight conditions within the selected aircraft’s operating limits. The project has not yet set a numerical wind limit, so that limit should be established from the aircraft’s guidance and flight-validation results.

C3. University site authorization. Campus approval and site boundaries govern where and when physical flight testing can occur. The requirement to submit an MSU UAS Flight Request at least three weeks in advance affects test scheduling, while approved activity boundaries may constrain route and test setup. We will include the request lead time in planning and proceed with campus flights only after approval. Bench, simulator, or replay work can support development when an approved flight is not available.

C4. Competition Specified sensor interface. The supplied picoScan150 and its variant-dependent accessories may constrain the acquisition interface and integration layout. A mismatch in the delivered unit’s pinout, profile configuration, or Ethernet connection could prevent operation or damage equipment. We will confirm the delivered variant and its documentation before finalizing the connection, then design to its specified four-pin D-coded M12 interface and 10/100 Mbit/s Ethernet capability as applicable.

C5. External electrical limits. The sensor’s required 9–30 V DC supply constrains power-system selection and integration. Supply compatibility, transient behavior, connector choice, and protective components affect reliable operation and could damage the sensor if inconsistent with its instructions. We will follow the delivered sensor documentation when selecting and integrating its supply, and confirm the flight platform’s allowable electrical limits and installed-mass capacity for the final configuration.

C6. Laser operating conditions. The sensor’s documented Class 1 classification depends on operation as specified by its manufacturer, so changes to the optical assembly or installation could invalidate the project’s safety assumptions. We will preserve the optical assembly and labeling, follow the manufacturer’s installation and service instructions, and avoid unauthorized optical modifications. This addresses the sensor’s specified operating conditions; it does not establish an independent laser certification for the finished UAV.
